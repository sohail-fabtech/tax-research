# Roadmap to Age 90 — How It Works

**A guide to what we ask a client, and what each answer changes.**

Written to be presented. Every number in this document comes from the system itself.

---

## The journey, on one page

A client answers questions in **eleven groups**. The system then runs **one year at a time, from today to age 90 or 100** — around fifty years — and produces four things.

```
WHAT WE ASK                     WHAT THE SYSTEM DOES              WHAT YOU SEE
──────────────                  ────────────────────              ────────────
 1  About you                                                     Income and tax,
 2  Family & retirement age     For each year:                    every year
 3  Income & business             what comes in
 4  Retirement accounts           what goes out                   What you can
 5  Social Security               what tax is due                 spend
 6  Property                      what is left
 7  Partnerships & investments     what it grows to               What you keep
 8  Insurance & estate                                            
 9  Living & medical costs       …then carry the answer           What reaches
10  Life events                   into next year                  your family
11  Tax savings & deployment
```

### Three rules that govern everything

**1. The order is part of the answer.** Forced retirement withdrawals must be added to income *before* tax is worked out. Contributions must be taken off *before*. Change the order and every number after it is wrong.

**2. We calculate in future dollars and show today's.** Tax brackets rise with inflation each year, but some thresholds have been frozen in law for decades. If we shrank the dollars first, we would compare shrunken income against frozen limits and understate the tax every later year.

**3. Answers carry forward.** A loss that cannot be used this year is not lost — it waits. Basis, unused losses and credits all move from one year to the next, sometimes for decades.

## 1. About you

**What we ask:** name, date of birth, filing status, spouse's details, state, and how far to project.

**What it changes:** these five answers set the tax brackets, the standard deduction, when forced withdrawals begin, and whether the state adds tax on top.

**The point where it matters**

| The answer | What happens |
|---|---|
| Born **1959 or earlier** | Forced retirement withdrawals start at **73** |
| Born **1960 or later** | They start at **75** — two extra years of low tax before they begin |
| Married filing jointly | Brackets are roughly **double** the single ones |
| Single | Same income, roughly **half** the bracket width — a materially higher rate |
| Living in **Texas, Florida, Nevada, Washington** and five others | **No state income tax** |
| Living in **California, New York, Oregon, Hawaii** | State tax on top, up to about **13%** |
| Estate in **Oregon** | State estate tax starts near **$1,000,000** — far below the federal line |
| Estate in **New York** | Exemption **$7,350,000**, but exceed **$7,717,500** and the *whole* estate is taxed |

*Steps driven: the tax step, the withdrawal step, and the estate step.*

> **Say this:** "Two people with identical money can end up in very different places purely on birth year and postcode."

## 2. Family and retirement age

**What we ask:** whether a dependent child lives with you, the age you plan to stop working, and whether you will work part-time afterwards.

**What it changes:** the retirement age is the hinge of the whole plan — the year salary stops and the savings start being spent.

**The point where it matters**

| The answer | What happens |
|---|---|
| The year you retire | Salary goes to zero; spending typically drops about **15%** |
| Retiring before **65** | You pay for your own health cover until Medicare starts |
| Working part-time before full retirement age | Benefits can be withheld — see Social Security below |
| A qualifying child at home | A surviving spouse keeps married-level brackets for two more years |
| **No** qualifying child | A surviving spouse moves to single rates the year after the death |

> **Say this:** "Most retirees have no dependent child, so a survivor goes to single rates almost immediately. That is the expensive path, and it is the common one."

*Steps driven: income, expenses, contributions.*

---

## 3. Income and business

**What we ask:** wages for you and your spouse, bonuses and share options, whether you own a business, what that business *does*, its profit, its payroll, and its equipment and property.

**What it changes:** this sets the tax bracket, the payroll tax, and whether the client qualifies for the 20% business deduction.

**The point where it matters**

| The answer | What happens |
|---|---|
| Wages up to **$184,500** | Social Security payroll tax applies; above that, only Medicare |
| Wages above **$250,000** (married) | An extra **0.9%** Medicare tax, and an extra **3.8%** on investment income |
| Business profit under **$403,500** (married) | Full 20% deduction, whatever the business does |
| **Service business** above **$553,500** | The 20% deduction is **gone entirely** |
| **Non-service business** at the same income | The deduction survives, limited by payroll and property |
| Business **loss** above **$512,000** (married) | The excess cannot be used this year; it carries forward |

**The single biggest swing in this category.** Same client, same $400,000 of business profit, same $250,000 salary:

| Business type | Deduction | Federal tax |
|---|---|---|
| A medical or dental practice | **$0** | **$151,039** |
| An engineering firm | **$80,000** | **$123,039** |

A **$28,000 difference in one year**, decided by profession alone.

**The law, in one sentence:** doctors, dentists, lawyers, accountants, consultants and financial advisers are "specified service businesses" and lose the deduction at higher income — but **engineers and architects were deliberately carved out** and keep it at any income.

> **Say this:** "Two identical businesses, identical profit. One keeps eighty thousand dollars of deduction and the other keeps nothing. The only difference is what they do for a living."

*Steps driven: income, the tax step, the business deduction, the investment surtax.*

---

## 4. Retirement accounts

**What we ask:** each account, its balance, what goes in each year, the employer's contribution, and last year's wages from that employer.

**What it changes:** contributions reduce today's tax and build the balance that will later be forced out and taxed.

**The point where it matters**

| Age or amount | What happens |
|---|---|
| Under **50** | Maximum into a 401(k): **$24,500** |
| **50 to 59** | **$32,500** |
| **60 to 63** | **$35,750** — the highest it ever gets |
| **64 onward** | Back down to **$32,500** |
| Last year's wages above **$150,000** | The catch-up part **must** be after-tax Roth — the deduction is gone |
| Roth accounts | **Never** forced out during your lifetime |

> **The mistake almost everyone makes:** assuming the ages 60–63 bonus adds to the age-50 bonus. It **replaces** it. The real number is $35,750, not $43,750 — and it drops at 64.

> **Say this:** "There are four years — 60 to 63 — when you can put in more than at any other point in your life. At 64 it drops back. Most people never know the window existed."

*Steps driven: contributions, growth, forced withdrawals.*

---

## 5. Social Security

**What we ask:** the benefit figure from the client's statement, and the age they intend to claim.

**What it changes:** claiming age is permanent. It cannot be revisited later.

**The point where it matters**

| Claim at | You receive | On our sample client |
|---|---|---|
| **62** | **70%** of the full amount, for life | $57,472 a year |
| **67** (full age) | **100%** | $66,128 a year |
| **70** | **124%** — the maximum | $72,363 a year |

**Two more rules that surprise people**

- **Working while claiming early.** Earn more than **$24,480** before full retirement age and **$1 is withheld for every $2** over. It is not lost — it comes back as a higher benefit later — but the cash flow changes.
- **When one spouse dies, the survivor keeps the higher of the two benefits, not both.** A couple receiving $96,105 between them does not leave the survivor with $96,105. They keep roughly **$62,567**.

**Taxation of the benefit.** Up to **85%** of Social Security can be taxable, decided by total income against limits of **$32,000 and $44,000** for a married couple. **Those limits have not moved since 1983 and never will** — which is why almost every retiree eventually reaches the 85% band.

> **Say this:** "Claiming at 62 instead of 67 is a permanent thirty percent cut. It is the single most irreversible decision in the plan."

*Steps driven: income, the tax step.*

## 6. Property

This is the category where two clients with identical properties get completely different answers.

**What we ask:** each property's price, land value, current value, mortgage, how it is let, the **average length of a guest's stay**, days rented, **days you use it yourself**, and your hours managing it.

**The three questions that decide everything**

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| How many days do you use it yourself? | Too many and no loss is allowed at all |
| What is the average guest stay? | **7 days or fewer** and it stops being a "rental" in tax terms |
| How many hours do you put in? | Over **100 hours**, and more than anyone else, usually counts as running it |

**Same property, same loss — the difference**

| How it is let | Taxable income | Federal tax |
|---|---|---|
| Short-term, **4-day** average stay | $206,164 | **$34,780** |
| Long-term lease | $347,800 | **$68,668** |

**A $33,888 difference in one year** on the identical building — because a short-stay property tested on hours can offset salary, while a long-let property cannot, no matter how hard you work it.

**And personal use can undo it entirely**

| Days you use it yourself | Federal tax |
|---|---|
| 6 days | **$34,780** |
| 30 days | **$82,768** |

**$47,988 of difference** from twenty-four nights.

**The law, in one sentence:** if personal use exceeds the greater of **14 days or 10% of the days it was rented**, deductions are capped at the rental income and no loss is allowed.

**Two more things to know**

- **A cost segregation study** moves part of the building into faster write-offs, producing a large deduction early. It is a **timing** strategy — the deduction is repaid as extra tax on sale, **unless the property is held until death**.
- **Bank lending counts** toward the amount you have at risk; **seller financing usually does not** — which can block a loss that otherwise looks fine.

> **Say this:** "Same building, same mortgage, same money. Let it in weeks instead of years and the loss becomes usable today rather than in a decade."

*Steps driven: property classification, all four loss tests, equity, the estate.*

## 7. Partnerships and investments

**What we ask:** each partnership or S-corp interest — your share of income and loss, your investment in it, how much you have at risk, and whether you actively run it. Plus your taxable investment accounts and any losses carried in from earlier years.

**What it changes:** a loss on paper is not a deduction. It has to pass **four tests, in order.**

| Test | The question | If it fails |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Investment | Have you put in that much? | Waits until you invest more |
| 2. Risk | Could you actually lose it? | Waits — borrowed money you aren't liable for doesn't count |
| 3. Participation | Do you actively run it? | Waits until that activity makes money, or you sell it |
| 4. Size | Is the loss over **$512,000**? | The excess carries forward to later years |

**The order matters.** A loss blocked at test 1 never reaches test 2. Each test only sees what the one above let through.

**Other limits worth naming**

| Item | Limit |
|---|---|
| Investment losses against ordinary income | **$3,000 a year** — unchanged since 1978 |
| Losses carried forward from earlier years | Can offset only **80%** of income in any later year |
| Partnership debt | **Adds** to what you can deduct |
| S-corporation company debt | **Does not** — only money you personally lent |

> **Say this:** "A client can have a real, genuine $120,000 loss and deduct none of it this year. Telling them otherwise is the fastest way to lose their trust when the return is filed."

*Steps driven: the four loss tests, income, the investment surtax.*

## 8. Insurance and estate

**What we ask:** each life policy — its value, cash value, premiums paid, any loan against it, and **who owns it**. Plus other assets, debts, and what the heirs' tax rate is likely to be.

**The point where it matters**

| The answer | What happens |
|---|---|
| Estate under **$15,000,000** per person | **No federal estate tax** |
| Above it | **40%** on the excess |
| Policy owned **personally** | The full death benefit counts toward the estate |
| Policy owned by a **trust** | It does not |
| Policy moved to a trust **within 3 years** of death | It counts anyway |
| Loans exceed the policy's value | The policy can collapse, creating a tax bill on money already spent |

**The point most plans miss.** Net worth is not what the family receives.

| $3,000,000 held as… | Heirs' income tax | They actually receive |
|---|---|---|
| An investment account | **$0** — the gain is wiped clean at death | **$3,000,000** |
| A retirement account | About **$1,050,000** | **$1,950,000** |

Identical on a statement. **Over a million dollars apart in reality.**

**The law, in one sentence:** most assets have their gain erased at death, but retirement accounts do not — the heirs pay income tax on every dollar.

> **Say this:** "Net worth is not what your family receives. Two accounts that look identical on a statement can be a million dollars apart once the tax is paid."

*Steps driven: the insurance roll-forward, the estate step.*

---

## 9. Living and medical costs

**What we ask:** monthly living costs, monthly medical costs, and whether to model a period of long-term care.

**What it changes:** this is what the plan has to fund. Get it wrong and everything downstream is wrong.

**The point where it matters**

| The answer | What happens |
|---|---|
| Medical costs | Grow at about **5% a year** — roughly double general inflation |
| Medical costs above **7.5% of income** | The excess becomes deductible |
| Long-term care | Typically **$75,000 to $150,000 a year** |
| Income above **$218,000** (married), from **two years ago** | Medicare premiums rise — and it is a cliff, not a slope |

> **Two things to point out.** Medical costs go from about **13% of the budget at 42 to 24% by 72** — a plan using one inflation rate misses that entirely. And a year of care is often the **lowest-tax year of a client's life**, because the deduction is so large — which makes it the cheapest year to move money into a Roth.

> **Say this:** "The year someone needs care is usually the most expensive year of their life and the cheapest year of tax. Those two facts together are a planning opportunity, not just a cost."

*Steps driven: expenses, the tax step, Medicare premiums.*

---

## 10. Life events

**What we ask:** whether to model the death of a spouse, a one-time windfall, a divorce, or annual Roth conversions.

**What it changes:** a fifty-year plan that assumes nothing ever happens is not a plan.

**The point where it matters**

| The event | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Death of a spouse** | Brackets, standard deduction and the Medicare threshold all roughly **halve** |
| A **windfall** | Medicare premiums rise **two years later** — and that cannot be appealed |
| **Divorce** | Filing status changes; accounts split |
| **Roth conversions** | Tax now, less forced income later |

**The death of a spouse is the biggest single swing in the system.** On our sample client:

| | Age 77 (both alive) | Age 79 (survivor) |
|---|---|---|
| Social Security | $96,105 | **$62,567** |
| Total income | $215,257 | $197,223 |
| **Federal tax** | **$21,677** | **$29,654** |

**Income fell 8%. Tax rose 37%.** The survivor is poorer and taxed harder at the same time.

> **The good news, and the reason to plan:** it is entirely predictable. Moving money into a Roth while both spouses are alive costs far less than the survivor would pay later.

> **Say this:** "A plan that assumes nothing ever happens over fifty years is not a plan. We model the things that actually happen to people."

*Steps driven: every step from that year onward.*

## 11. Tax savings and how they are deployed

**What we ask:** the approved tax savings from the strategies, what percentage is deployed, and how it is split.

**What it changes:** this is the engine of the roadmap. Savings that are not deployed are just a lower tax bill; savings that are deployed become capital.

**How it works**

```
Approved tax savings  +  a percentage of the deduction base  =  deployed every year
        $188,000      +        20% of $420,000              =        $272,000
```

That money is then split across **six buckets**, each growing at its own rate:

| Bucket | Typical share |
|---|---|
| Retirement accounts | 35% |
| Life insurance cash value | 25% |
| Real estate and business | 25% |
| Liquidity reserve | 10% |
| Legacy and philanthropy | 3% |
| Disability and long-term care cover | 2% |

The percentages must total 100%. The recommended deployment band is **15% to 25%**.

> **Say this:** "A tax saving you spend is a smaller bill. A tax saving you deploy becomes capital that compounds for fifty years."

*Steps driven: growth, and every balance in the plan.*

## The numbers that trigger something

Keep this page open in a meeting.

| Threshold | What happens at it | Rises with inflation? |
|---|---|---|
| Born 1960 or later | Forced withdrawals start at 75, not 73 | — |
| **$24,500** | Standard 401(k) limit | Yes |
| **$35,750** | The most anyone can put in, ages 60–63 only | Yes |
| **$150,000** last year's wages | Catch-up contributions must be Roth | No |
| **$24,480** earnings | $1 of benefit withheld per $2 over, before full retirement age | Yes |
| **$32,000 / $44,000** | Social Security starts being taxed, then up to 85% | **Never — frozen since 1983** |
| **$184,500** wages | Social Security payroll tax stops | Yes |
| **$250,000** income | 3.8% investment surtax begins | **Never — frozen since 2013** |
| **$403,500 → $553,500** | The 20% business deduction phases out for service businesses | Yes |
| **$512,000** business loss | The excess is pushed into future years | Yes |
| **$218,000** income, two years back | Medicare premiums step up — a cliff, not a slope | Yes |
| **7 days** average stay | A property stops being a "rental" and can offset salary | — |
| **14 days** personal use | Deductions capped at rental income | — |
| **7.5% of income** | Medical costs above this become deductible | — |
| **$3,000** | Investment losses usable against ordinary income each year | **Never — since 1978** |
| **$15,000,000** | Federal estate tax begins | Yes |

> **Point out the frozen ones.** Four limits have not moved in decades. Every year, more clients cross them without earning any more in real terms. That is not a flaw in the plan — it is the law working as written, and it is why the plan has to look forward fifty years.

## Six clients, start to finish

### 1. Salaried couple, 45 — the simple path

Two salaries totalling $275,000, one retirement account, no business, no property, Texas.

| | |
|---|---|
| Year one tax | **$59,120** (21.5% of income) |
| Net worth at 65 | **$6,286,487** |
| At 90 | **$16,751,627**, leaving **$14,780,493** |
| What fires | Medicare surcharge at 65; forced withdrawals of $222,617 at 75 |

Most categories are skipped. Their plan turns on retirement age and claiming age.

### 2. Practice owner, 52 — the service business penalty

$250,000 salary plus $400,000 of practice profit, California.

| | |
|---|---|
| Year one tax | **$222,833** (34.3% of income) |
| Business deduction | **$0** — a health practice is a service business |
| The same profit in engineering | Would have given **$80,000** of deduction and saved **$28,000** |
| What else fires | Wages over $150,000, so catch-up must be Roth |

### 3. Short-term rental investor, 40 — the rule that changes everything

$380,000 salary, one $900,000 property let on 4-day average stays, 340 hours a year of self-management.

| | |
|---|---|
| Year one federal tax | **$34,780** |
| The same property on long lets | **$68,668** |
| The difference | **$33,888 in one year** |
| Net worth at 60 | **$11,640,554** |

The 7-day rule plus the hours turn a suspended loss into one that offsets salary now.

### 4. Landlord with long leases, 58 — the same loss, waiting

Identical property, let on 12-month leases.

| | |
|---|---|
| Year one federal tax | **$95,482** |
| The loss | Real, but suspended — hours make no difference |
| Net worth at 67 | **$3,483,363** |

> The contrast between clients 3 and 4 is the single best story in the system: same building, same money, different lease length.

### 5. Business owner selling at 63 — the tax that arrives late

$4,000,000 sale with a $600,000 basis, Florida.

| | |
|---|---|
| Tax in the year of sale | **$1,066,810** |
| Of which alternative minimum tax | **$319,070**, with a credit carried forward |
| Two years later | Medicare premiums rise, based on the sale year |
| Net worth at 92 | **$11,772,452** |

The surcharge two years after the sale **cannot be appealed**. The only defence is timing the sale.

### 6. Widow at 78 — the biggest swing

$2,000,000 retirement account, both benefits, Colorado. Spouse dies at 78.

| | Age 77 | Age 79 |
|---|---|---|
| Social Security | $96,105 | **$62,567** |
| Income | $215,257 | $197,223 |
| **Federal tax** | $21,677 | **$29,654** |

**Income −8%, tax +37%.** Filing status stays married for the whole year of death, then moves to single.

## Presenting this

**A 60-second opening**

> "We ask about eleven areas of your life. Then we run your finances forward one year at a time to 90 or 100 — around fifty years — and show what you earn, what you pay, what you can spend, and what reaches your family.
>
> The value is not the final number. It is seeing which decisions actually move it, and when. A short lease instead of a long one. Claiming at 67 instead of 62. Converting while both spouses are alive."

**The five answers that change the most**

1. The **average length of a guest's stay** at a rental property
2. **What the business does** — service or not
3. The age Social Security is **claimed**
4. **Birth year** — it sets when forced withdrawals begin
5. Whether a **spouse's death** is modelled

**Three things not to promise**

- That the projection is a prediction. It shows what happens *if* the assumptions hold.
- That a strategy works before an advisor has approved the tax savings behind it.
- That the law will stay put. Several of these figures change every autumn, and four have been frozen for decades.
