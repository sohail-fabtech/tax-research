# THE PEOPLE

### Case 1 — Client's date of birth

**Born 10 May 1984**
- born after 1959, so forced withdrawals start at **75**, not 73
- two extra low-tax years before the government forces income
- Social Security first available at 62, full at 67, maximum at 70
- Medicare at 65 — but priced on his income at **63**
- catch-up opens at 50, a bigger one at 60–63
- one wrong date moves every one of these together

*What each age is worth to him — claiming factors per SSA, FRA 67*

| Age | What happens | For this client |
|---|---|---|
| 62 | Social Security first available | **$2,906/mo** — 70% |
| 63 | — | $3,114/mo — 75% |
| 65 | Medicare starts, priced on his income at 63 | Part B $202.90, or $284.10 over the line |
| 67 | Full retirement age | **$4,152/mo** — 100% |
| 70 | Maximum | **$5,148/mo** — 124% |
| 75 | Forced withdrawals begin | balance ÷ 24.6 |

Claiming at 70 rather than 62 is **77% more a month, for life.**

**Law:** IRC §401(a)(9)(C)(v) with SECURE 2.0 §107 — applicable age; §63(f) age-65 addition; §151(d)(5)(C) senior deduction; §414(v) catch-up; Social Security Act §202(q) claiming adjustment

**Held today:** yes

### Case 2 — Filing status

**Married filing jointly**
- status is fixed by where he stands on **31 December**, not when the year started
- sets the bracket table and the standard deduction
- sets four separate thresholds, and each one halves if he becomes single
- nothing downstream can be worked out until this is known

*IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 — 2026 standard deduction*

| Filing status | Standard deduction |
|---|---|
| Married filing jointly | $32,200 |
| Single | $16,100 |
| Head of household | $24,150 |
| Married filing separately | $16,100 |

**Law:** IRC §1 rate schedules; §63(c) standard deduction; §2 surviving spouse and head of household; §7703 status fixed at year end

**Held today:** yes

### Case 3 — Spouse, 41, no job

**Her own benefit $2,333/mo · 50% of his = $2,076/mo**
- she receives the **larger** of the two, never both
- waiting past her full age adds nothing to a spousal benefit
- she has no work plan, so her IRA deduction range is the higher one
- if he dies first, she keeps his $4,152 and loses her own — see case 27

*IRS 2026 IRA deduction phase-out — Notice 2025-67*

| Situation | Phase-out range |
|---|---|
| Spouse covered by a work plan | $129,000 – $149,000 |
| Spouse **not** covered, other spouse is | $242,000 – $252,000 |

*What she could claim on his record, against her own $2,333*

| Her option | Amount |
|---|---|
| Spousal at 62 | $1,349/mo — 32.5% of his benefit |
| Spousal at 65 | $1,730/mo |
| Spousal at her full age 67 | $2,076/mo — 50%, and waiting longer adds nothing |
| **Her own benefit** | **$2,333/mo — she takes this** |

The spousal reduction is 25/36 of 1% a month, a **different formula** from the worker's own 5/9.

**Law:** Social Security Act §202(b) spousal; §202(k) dual entitlement — benefits never add; IRC §219(g)(7) uncovered spouse

**Held today:** partly — we hold her date of birth, benefit and claiming age, but not her own accounts

### Case 4 — Two children, 16 and 8

**Stored as `children = 2` and one age**
- each child is entitled to **50%** of his $4,152 while he lives, **75%** after he dies
- but the family maximum caps the household total, and here it bites hard
- spouse and two children want $6,228 between them, and only $3,113 is available
- so each of them is cut to about half — **$1,038/mo, not $2,076**
- the benefit stops at 18, or 19 if still at school
- both children share one age in our system, so they age out on the same day
- one of them is eight years wrong

*Family maximum on his $4,152 benefit — Federal Register 2025-19763 bend points*

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Family maximum | $7,265/mo |
| less his own benefit | $4,152 |
| **left for spouse + 2 children** | **$3,113** |
| what the three want at 50% each | $6,228 |
| so each is cut to 50.0% | **$1,038/mo each** |

**And worse** — the sample data writes `dependents`, the engine reads `depChildren`
- the keys do not match
- these two children currently produce **$0**

**Law:** Social Security Act §202(d) children's benefit; **§203(a) family maximum**

**Held today:** no — a count and one shared age

### Case 5 — Child 1 turns 18 next year

**Child 1 = 18 · Child 2 = 9**
- Child 1's benefit stops at 18, or 19 if still at school
- Child 2 keeps drawing for another ten years
- the family maximum then re-spreads
- so Child 2's monthly share can go **up** when Child 1 drops off

| | Both children | Child 2 alone |
|---|---|---|
| Claimants sharing the room | spouse + 2 children | spouse + 1 child |
| Room available | $3,113/mo | $3,113/mo |
| Each receives | ~$1,038 | ~$1,557 |

**Law:** Social Security Act §202(d)(1) — ends at 18, or 19 while a full-time school student

**Held today:** no — with one shared age we cannot stop one child and keep the other

### Case 6 — Child 1 is 20 and in college

**Age 20 · full-time student · living at home in term breaks**
- for **tax**, still a qualifying child up to 24 while a full-time student
- for **Social Security**, the children's benefit already stopped at 19
- two different rules, two different ages
- people assume one answer covers both

| Rule | Age limit for this child |
|---|---|
| Tax — qualifying child | **24**, while a full-time student |
| Social Security children's benefit | **19** — already stopped |

**Law:** IRC §152(c) qualifying child, to 24 for a student; Social Security Act §202(d) stops at 19

**Held today:** no student flag, no support test

### Case 7 — Adult child, 26, at home, no income

**Age 26 · no income · client pays everything**
- too old to be a qualifying child — that ends at 19, or 24 for a student
- so the qualifying-relative test applies instead
- that one turns on **his income**, not his age
- client must also provide more than half his total support

| Test | Result at 26 |
|---|---|
| Qualifying child | **fails** — past 19, and past 24 for a student |
| Qualifying relative | possible — turns on **his gross income**, not his age |
| Support | client must pay more than **half** the total |

**Law:** IRC §152(c) qualifying child; §152(d) qualifying relative

**Held today:** no

### Case 8 — Supporting a parent in their own home

**Parent lives elsewhere · client pays most of the bills**
- a child must live with the client
- a **parent does not** — the living-together test does not apply here
- the parent's own gross income is what decides it
- client must provide more than half the support

| | A child | A parent |
|---|---|---|
| Must live with the client? | **Yes** | **No** |
| Test turns on | age and student status | gross income |
| Support test | applies | applies |

**Law:** IRC §152(d) — a parent is on the relationship list and is not subject to the shared-home test

**Held today:** no — there is no dependant type at all, only a child count

### Case 9 — A child with a permanent disability

**Disability began before age 22**
- dependency test has **no age limit** at all
- Social Security child's benefit can continue **past 18**, for life
- on inheritance she is an eligible designated beneficiary
- so the **10-year rule does not apply** — the account stretches over her own life
- three separate rules, all switched on by one field we do not hold

| Rule | Normal child | Disabled child |
|---|---|---|
| Dependency | ends at 19, or 24 if a student | **no age limit** |
| Social Security | ends at 18 / 19 | **continues for life** |
| Inherits the 401(k) | 10 years to empty | **stretch over her own life** |

**Law:** IRC §152(c)(3)(B); Social Security Act §202(d) disability beginning before 22; SECURE Act P.L. 116-94 — eligible designated beneficiary

**Held today:** no — the single most expensive missing field in the family section

# OWNERSHIP AND BENEFICIARY

### Case 11 — House in the spouse's name only

**Title = spouse alone**
- at the first death it passes through **her** estate, not his
- only she can sell it, refinance it, or move it into a trust
- in California it is likely community property anyway, which changes the step-up — case 16

**Law:** IRC §2033 — the estate includes property the decedent owned. IRS Publication 555 — in California, property acquired during the marriage is generally community property whatever the title says.

**Held today:** no — property has 24 fields and none is an owner

### Case 12 — Rental owned half with a brother

**50% owner, not 100%**
- value $1,300,000 → his half $650,000
- mortgage $800,000 → his half $400,000
- equity in his estate $500,000 → **$250,000**
- the K-1 loss, the basis and the at-risk amount all halve too
- every property number in the plan is currently double what it should be

**Law:** IRC §2033 brings only his share into the estate; §704(d) and §465 measure basis and at-risk on his share alone

**Held today:** no — the system takes the whole property as his

### Case 13 — 401(k) beneficiary is still the ex-wife

**Beneficiary form on file = ex-wife · will says children · he is remarried**
- **the beneficiary form beats the will** — the will does not redirect the account
- but a 401(k) protects the **current** spouse by federal law
- the balance must go to her unless she signed a written waiver, witnessed by a notary or plan representative
- an **IRA has no such protection** — there the ex-wife really does receive it
- nobody finds out until after death, when it cannot be fixed

| Account | Who actually receives it |
|---|---|
| **401(k)** $900,000 | the **current spouse** — unless she signed a witnessed waiver |
| **IRA** $200,000 | the **ex-wife** — the form controls, no spousal protection |

**Law:** IRC §401(a)(11), §417; Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)-20 — spousal consent. The plan fiduciary must pay per the plan documents and the beneficiary form.

**Held today:** no — there is not one beneficiary field anywhere in the system

The most common and most costly mistake in real practice, and we cannot even record it.

---

### Case 14 — IRA beneficiary says "my estate"

**Beneficiary = the estate, not a person**
- naming a person allows a 10-year payout, spread as the heir chooses
- the estate is **not an individual**, so there is no designated beneficiary at all
- where he dies before his required beginning age of 75, the **5-year rule** applies instead
- five years to empty $200,000, not ten — roughly double the income each year
- and the account is dragged through probate

| Beneficiary | If he dies before 75 |
|---|---|
| A person | **10 years** to empty |
| **The estate** — not an individual | **5 years** to empty |

**Law:** IRS Publication 590-B — "If the owner's beneficiary isn't an individual (for example, if the beneficiary is the owner's estate), the 5-year rule applies."

**Held today:** no

---

### Case 15 — Owner, insured and beneficiary

**Policy $3,000,000 · owner = the client himself**
- owner is whoever can change the beneficiary, cash it in, borrow on it, or transfer it
- owned personally, the full $3,000,000 is added to his estate
- owned by a trust from the start, it is **$0** in the estate
- at the 40% estate rate that is up to **$1,200,000** of difference
- income tax is free either way — estate tax is the whole question

| Owner | In the estate | Estate tax at 40% |
|---|---|---|
| **Client personally** | $3,000,000 | up to **$1,200,000** |
| Trust, from the start | $0 | **$0** |
| Trust, but transferred within 3 years of death | $3,000,000 | up to $1,200,000 |

**Law:** IRC §2042 incidents of ownership; §2035(a) three-year rule; §101(a)(1) income-tax exclusion

**Held today:** owner yes — the only ownership field in the system. Beneficiary no.

### Case 16 — Joint account in California

**$600,000 account · $400,000 basis · $200,000 gain · California**
- in most states only the deceased half steps up → $100,000 of gain erased
- California is a **community property** state
- both halves step up → **$200,000** of gain erased
- the children inherit with zero taxable gain instead of $100,000
- the state is held in our system, but nothing uses it for this

| | Gain erased | Left taxable |
|---|---|---|
| Most states — half steps up | $100,000 | $100,000 |
| **California — both halves step up** | **$200,000** | **$0** |

**Law:** IRC §1014(b)(6) — in a community property state both halves are adjusted on the first death, not merely the decedent's. IRS Publication 555.

**Held today:** no owner, no title type

# THE MONEY

### Case 17 — Salary $420,000 plus S-corp profit $180,000

**S corporation · own W-2 $150,000 · profit $180,000**
- payroll tax hits the **$150,000 of wages**, not the profit share
- his 401(k) contribution is capped by the **wages**
- the 20% business deduction runs off the **profit**
- but consulting is a service business, so it is gone entirely above the threshold

*IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 — 2026 §199A thresholds*

| Filing status | Full deduction below | Fully phased out above |
|---|---|---|
| Married filing jointly | $403,500 | $553,500 |
| All others | $201,750 | $276,750 |

**Law:** IRC §1402 self-employment income; §199A(b) and §199A(d)(2) — consulting is a specified service trade, so the deduction is lost above the range

**Held today:** yes — entity, field, profit and own W-2 are all captured

### Case 18 — K-1 loss of $120,000

**Loss $120,000 · basis $80,000 · at risk $50,000 · he does not work in the business**
- gate 1, basis: allows $80,000, parks $40,000
- gate 2, at risk: allows $50,000, parks $30,000
- gate 3, passive: allows **$0**, parks $50,000
- the $25,000 rental allowance is gone above $150,000 of income, and he earns $420,000
- gate 4 is never reached
- **deducted this year $0**, parked $120,000 in three separate piles
- each pile is freed by a different event, so they are not interchangeable

| Gate | Allows | Parks | Freed by |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 · basis $80,000 | $80,000 | $40,000 | rebuilding basis |
| 2 · at risk $50,000 | $50,000 | $30,000 | putting more at risk |
| 3 · passive | **$0** | $50,000 | passive income, or selling the whole interest |
| 4 · overall cap | not reached | — | — |

**Law:** IRC §704(d) basis → §465 at risk → §469 passive → §461(l) overall cap, in that order; §469(i) sets the $25,000 allowance and its $100,000–$150,000 phase-out

**Held today:** yes, but never shown — the three piles are calculated and then not displayed

### Case 19 — The rental's 5-day average stay

**Average stay 5 days · his 320 hours against the manager's 40**
- 7 days or less, and he does the work, so the loss is **not passive**
- it can offset his $420,000 salary
- it must still clear the at-risk gate
- push the average to 8 days and the **whole year** fails — not just that booking
- the property reverts to passive for every day of it

| Average stay | Passive? | Offsets his $420,000 salary? |
|---|---|---|
| **5 days** + he does the work | **no** | **yes** |
| 7.4 days | yes | no — the whole year fails |
| 5 days, but a manager does the work | yes | no |

**Law:** Treas. Reg. §1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii)(A) — average stay of seven days or less is not a rental activity; §1.469-5T(a) material participation

**Held today:** yes — average stay, own hours and other hours are all captured

### Case 20 — Traditional $900,000 and Roth $200,000

**Same statement, two very different assets**
- Traditional drives the forced withdrawal at 75; Roth never does
- Traditional reaches the children as ordinary income; Roth reaches them tax-free
- Traditional gets **no** step-up at death
- at a 35% heir rate, $900,000 of Traditional is worth **$585,000** to a child
- $600,000 of brokerage is worth the full $600,000
- the bigger number is worth less

| | Traditional $900,000 | Roth $200,000 | Taxable $600,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Forced withdrawal at 75 | **yes** | no | no |
| Step-up at death | **none** | n/a | **full** |
| A child receives | **$585,000** | $200,000 | **$600,000** |

**Law:** IRC §401(a)(9) forced withdrawals; §402A Roth; §691 income in respect of a decedent; §1014(c) denies the step-up to retirement money

**Held today:** type yes, owner no — we cannot tell whose IRA it is

### Case 21 — Taxable $600,000, basis $400,000

**Only $200,000 has ever been gain**
- that $200,000 is the only part that can be taxed
- at death the step-up erases it
- the child receives the full $600,000 with nothing owed
- best asset to leave to a child, worst to spend first

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Balance | $600,000 |
| Basis | $400,000 |
| Gain that could ever be taxed | $200,000 |
| **At death, after the step-up** | **$0 taxable — the child receives all $600,000** |

**Law:** IRC §1014(a) — basis becomes the value at death

**Held today:** balance and basis yes, title no — see case 16

### Case 22 — The policy, $54,400 a year in

**Cash value $350,000 · premiums paid $300,000 · credited 5.5% · loan rate 5%**
- while the policy stays alive, loans are tax-free and stay out of AGI
- so they do not make his Social Security taxable and do not raise his Medicare bill
- unpaid loan interest compounds into the balance
- if the loan ever passes the cash value, the policy **lapses**
- the $350,000 is then treated as paid out, less $300,000 of premiums
- **$50,000 taxable, with no cash received**, and the $3,000,000 benefit is gone

| | While it lives | If it lapses |
|---|---|---|
| Cash value $350,000 | borrowed tax-free | treated as paid out |
| Premiums paid $300,000 | — | subtracted as basis |
| Taxable | **$0** | **$50,000** |
| Death benefit | $3,000,000 | **gone** |

**Law:** IRC §72(e) — a lapse with a loan outstanding is a deemed distribution of the cash value; §7702 defines the contract

**Held today:** yes — the engine tests for the lapse every year and writes a warning

### Case 26 — Who decides what

**The picture's own rule: the front end renders approved values**

# THE FAR END

### Case 27 — Spouse dies at 80

**Household benefit $4,152 + $2,333 → $4,152 only**
- she keeps the larger benefit, not both — the household loses $2,333 a month
- filing status becomes single the following year
- joint rates continue two more years only while a dependent child lives at home, and by 80 there is none
- every bracket roughly halves — the 22% band ends at $211,400 joint but $105,700 single
- Social Security starts being taxed at $25,000 instead of $32,000
- the Medicare surcharge starts at $109,000 instead of $218,000
- spending only falls to 75%, while income falls much further

| | Married | Widowed |
|---|---|---|
| Household benefit | $4,152 + $2,333 | **$4,152 only** |
| 22% band ends | $211,400 | $105,700 |
| Social Security taxed from | $32,000 | $25,000 |
| Medicare surcharge from | $218,000 | $109,000 |
| Investment tax from | $250,000 | $200,000 |

Worked example: income **down 12%**, federal tax **up 29.5%**, cash after tax **down 16.9%**.

**Law:** IRC §6013(a)(3) joint return for the year of death; §2(a) surviving spouse needs a dependent child; §1(j)(2) single rates; §86(c) and §1411 thresholds; Social Security Act §202(e) survivor benefit

**Held today:** yes — the death can be modelled, with the year and which spouse

### Case 29 — Client dies, what does each child get?

**Statement total looks the same. What arrives does not.**
- Traditional 401(k) $900,000 → ordinary income, ten years to empty, no step-up → **$585,000** at 35%
- Roth IRA $200,000 → tax-free, still ten years → **$200,000**
- Taxable $600,000 → basis steps up, gain erased → **$600,000**
- Rental property → basis steps up, depreciation recapture erased
- Life insurance $3,000,000 → no income tax, but **inside** the estate because he owns it
- Parked K-1 losses $120,000 → **mostly extinguished**

$900,000 of IRA is worth less than $600,000 of brokerage. The statement says otherwise.

| Asset | Value | Child receives | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional 401(k) | $900,000 | **$585,000** | ordinary income at 35%, no step-up |
| Roth IRA | $200,000 | $200,000 | tax-free |
| Taxable account | $600,000 | $600,000 | step-up erases the gain |
| Life insurance | $3,000,000 | $3,000,000 | no income tax — but inside the estate |
| Parked K-1 losses | $120,000 | **$0** | mostly extinguished |

**Law:** IRC §1014 step-up; §1014(c) and §691 deny it to retirement money; §101(a) insurance exclusion; SECURE Act P.L. 116-94 ten-year rule

**Held today:** no — every heir gets the same assumed 35%, and no account has a named beneficiary, so we cannot say who receives which asset
